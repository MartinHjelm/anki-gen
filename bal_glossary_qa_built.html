Water column What is the water column? The vertical extent of water from the sea surface down to the seafloor, treated as a one-dimensional frame for depth-varying properties.
Water column What properties vary along the water column? Temperature, salinity, light, and plankton concentration.

Practical Salinity Unit (PSU) What is the typical salinity of standard open-ocean seawater? About 35 PSU.

Adiabatic process What is an adiabatic process? A thermodynamic change in which a parcel exchanges no heat with its surroundings; temperature change comes only from work done against pressure.
Potential temperature What is potential temperature? The temperature a water parcel would have if moved adiabatically (without exchanging heat) to the surface pressure level.
Potential temperature What is the ocean-model variable name for potential temperature? thetao.
Wind stress What is wind stress? The horizontal force per unit area (N m⁻²) that the atmosphere exerts on the sea surface through friction.
Convective cooling What is convective cooling? Loss of heat from the surface ocean to a colder atmosphere, making surface water denser so it sinks.
Mixed layer What is the mixed layer? The near-surface ocean layer actively stirred by wind and convection, with nearly uniform temperature and density.
Mixed layer What is the NEMO variable name for mixed-layer depth? mlotst.
Stratification What is stratification? Stable vertical layering of the water column into distinct density layers, with lighter water floating on denser water.
Upwelling What is upwelling? The vertical transport of cool, nutrient-rich deeper water to the ocean surface.
Sea-ice concentration What is sea-ice concentration? The fraction of the sea surface covered by ice at a given location, from 0 (ice-free) to 1 (fully covered).
Anoxia & Hypoxia What is hypoxia? Dissolved oxygen below ~2 mg/L — too low to support most marine life.
Anoxia & Hypoxia What is anoxia? Complete absence of dissolved oxygen.
Baltic Inflow (Major Baltic Inflow) What is a Major Baltic Inflow? A periodic inflow of dense, saline North Sea water through the Danish Straits along the seafloor into the deep Baltic basins.
Anthropogenic What does "anthropogenic" mean? Caused or substantially modified by human activity, as opposed to occurring naturally.
Anthropogenic What is the primary cause of present-day Baltic eutrophication? Anthropogenic nutrient inputs from agriculture, wastewater, and atmospheric deposition.
Catchment (drainage basin) What is a catchment? The total land area whose surface and subsurface water all drain into a single river, lake, or sea.

Phosphate (PO₄³⁻) What is the primary inorganic phosphorus form available to phytoplankton? Phosphate (PO₄³⁻).
Silicate (Si(OH)₄) What is silicate, and which organisms need it? Dissolved silica required by diatoms to build their glass-like cell walls (frustules).
Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) What is hydrogen sulfide and where is it produced? A toxic gas produced by sulfate-reducing bacteria in fully anoxic water and sediments.
Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) Which Baltic biogeochemical model tracks H₂S as a state variable? ERGOM.
Liebig's Law of the Minimum What does Liebig's Law of the Minimum state? Biological growth is constrained by the single scarcest essential resource, not by the total amount of resources available.
Limiting nutrient What is a limiting nutrient? The single nutrient whose supply restricts phytoplankton growth below its maximum possible rate.
Internal loading What is internal loading? The release of phosphorus stored in bottom sediments back into the water column, driven by anoxia rather than river inputs.
Eutrophication What is eutrophication? The process by which a water body becomes enriched with nutrients, causing excessive algal growth, oxygen depletion, and reduced biodiversity.
Photosynthesis What is photosynthesis? A light-driven process that converts CO₂ and water into sugars and O₂, powered by pigments such as chlorophyll-a.
Pigment What is a pigment? A molecule that selectively absorbs particular wavelengths of light and reflects or transmits the rest.
Pigment What is the dominant pigment in phytoplankton? Chlorophyll-a.
Biomass What is biomass in plankton ecology? The total mass of living material in a defined volume or area — the standard currency for quantifying a plankton population.
Biomass What units are typically used to quantify phytoplankton biomass? Milligrams of chlorophyll, milligrams of carbon, or cell counts per cubic metre.
Taxonomy What is taxonomy? The formal hierarchical classification of organisms into ranks such as kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species.
Prokaryote What is a prokaryote? A single-celled organism whose DNA is not enclosed in a membrane-bound nucleus.
Prokaryote Name three groups of prokaryotes. Bacteria, archaea, and photosynthetic cyanobacteria.

Protists What are protists? Single-celled eukaryotic microorganisms — microbes with a true membrane-bound nucleus, in contrast to prokaryotes.
Flagella (flagellum) What are flagella? Long whip- or thread-like cellular appendages used for locomotion in many microorganisms.
Flagella (flagellum) What is the difference between a flagellum and flagella? Flagellum is the singular form; flagella is the plural.
Frustule What is a frustule? The two-piece silica (glass) cell wall of a diatom.
Phytoplankton What are phytoplankton? Microscopic photosynthetic organisms that drift in the water column, forming the base of marine food webs.
Chlorophyll-a What is chlorophyll-a? The primary green pigment used by all phytoplankton for photosynthesis.
Phycocyanin What is phycocyanin? A blue pigment unique to cyanobacteria, distinct from the chlorophyll-a present in all phytoplankton.
Phycocyanin Which OLCI band targets phycocyanin? RRS620.
Phytoplankton functional types (PFTs) What are phytoplankton functional types (PFTs)? A classification of phytoplankton into groups by ecological role and optical signature rather than strict taxonomy.
Picoplankton What is picoplankton? The smallest phytoplankton size class, typically less than 2 µm in diameter, including tiny prokaryotic cyanobacteria.
Nanophytoplankton What is nanophytoplankton? Mid-sized phytoplankton cells in the 2–20 µm range, including cryptophytes and green algae.
Nanophytoplankton What CMEMS variable represents nanophytoplankton? NANO (CRYPTO + half of GREEN).
Microphytoplankton What are microphytoplankton? The largest phytoplankton cells (>20 µm), composed primarily of diatoms and dinoflagellates.
Microphytoplankton What CMEMS variable represents microphytoplankton? MICRO (DIATO + DINO).
Diatoms What are diatoms? Single-celled algae encased in an intricate glass-like silica shell called a frustule.
Cyanobacteria What are cyanobacteria? Photosynthetic bacteria — not true algae — capable of fixing atmospheric nitrogen gas.
Dinoflagellates What are dinoflagellates? Flagellated single-celled protists characterised by two perpendicular flagella.
Cryptophytes What are cryptophytes? Small flagellated algae common in brackish coastal water and near river outflows, tolerant of low salinity and turbidity.
Cryptophytes Which CMEMS variable tracks cryptophyte chlorophyll? CRYPTO.
Flagellates What are flagellates? Organisms that propel themselves using one or more whip-like appendages called flagella.
Nitrogen fixation What is nitrogen fixation? The biological conversion of inert dissolved N₂ gas into bioavailable ammonia.
Cyanotoxins What are cyanotoxins? Bioactive toxins produced by certain cyanobacteria.
Crustaceans What are crustaceans? Arthropods with a hard chitinous exoskeleton and multiple pairs of jointed limbs.
Crustaceans Give examples of crustaceans. Copepods, krill, amphipods, crabs, and barnacles.
Zooplankton What is zooplankton? Animal-like plankton ranging from single-celled protists to small crustaceans that graze on phytoplankton and bacteria.
Copepods What are copepods? Small crustaceans and the most abundant multicellular animals in the sea.
Primary production What is primary production? The synthesis of organic carbon (biomass) from inorganic CO₂ by photosynthetic organisms.
Primary production What units quantify net primary production? mg C m⁻³ day⁻¹.
Trophic state What is trophic state? A classification of water bodies by their nutrient richness and associated biological productivity.
Oligotrophic What does oligotrophic mean? Low in dissolved nutrients, low in phytoplankton biomass, and consequently very clear and transparent water.
Algal bloom What is an algal bloom? A rapid, large increase in phytoplankton abundance.
Spring bloom What is the spring bloom? The first major phytoplankton growth event of the year, triggered by increasing solar irradiance after winter.
Summer bloom (cyanobacteria bloom) Which organisms dominate the Baltic summer bloom? Filamentous cyanobacteria such as Nodularia spumigena, Aphanizomenon flos-aquae, and Dolichospermum spp.
Grazing What is grazing in plankton ecology? Consumption of phytoplankton and bacteria by zooplankton such as copepods, ciliates, and heterotrophic flagellates.
Ciliates What are ciliates? Single-celled protists covered in hundreds to thousands of short hair-like cilia used for locomotion and feeding.
Wavelength (nanometre, nm) What is wavelength? The distance between successive crests of an electromagnetic wave; it determines the colour of light.
Wavelength (nanometre, nm) What is the visible-light wavelength range in nm? Approximately 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red).
Radiance & Irradiance What is radiance? The intensity of light arriving from or leaving in a single specified direction (W m⁻² sr⁻¹).
Radiance & Irradiance What is irradiance? The total light flux per unit area summed over all directions (W m⁻²).
Remote-sensing reflectance (Rrs) What is remote-sensing reflectance (Rrs)? The ratio of upwelling radiance leaving the ocean surface to downwelling solar irradiance, measured in sr⁻¹.
Inherent Optical Properties (IOPs) What are inherent optical properties (IOPs)? Optical properties of water that depend only on the dissolved and particulate substances present, independent of the ambient light field.
Backscattering What is backscattering? The fraction of incident light scattered back toward the light source by particles suspended in the water.
CDOM (Coloured Dissolved Organic Matter) What is CDOM? Yellow-brown dissolved organic material leached from soils and vegetation, carried to the sea by rivers — sometimes called "yellow substance" or Gelbstoff.
Diffuse attenuation coefficient (Kd) What does the diffuse attenuation coefficient Kd measure? How rapidly downwelling light intensity decreases with depth, in units of m⁻¹.
Diffuse attenuation coefficient (Kd) What does KD490 specifically measure? Light attenuation at 490 nm (blue-green light).
Secchi depth What is Secchi depth? The depth at which a white disk lowered into the water becomes invisible to an observer on the surface — a simple measure of water transparency.
Band-ratio algorithm What is a band-ratio algorithm? An empirical satellite ocean-colour algorithm that estimates chlorophyll-a from the ratio of remote-sensing reflectance at two wavelengths.
QAA (Quasi-Analytical Algorithm) What is the Quasi-Analytical Algorithm (QAA)? A semi-analytical inversion that derives inherent optical properties from Rrs by inverting the physics of underwater light propagation.
Fluorescence line height What is fluorescence line height? A measure of chlorophyll fluorescence emitted by phytoplankton in a narrow band around 683–685 nm when illuminated by sunlight.
Fluorescence line height Which OLCI band is the fluorescence band? RRS681_25.
In-situ measurement What is an in-situ measurement? A direct measurement made at the location of interest by an instrument physically present there, in contrast to remote sensing.
Swath What is a satellite swath? The strip of the Earth's surface imaged in a single satellite pass, set by the sensor's field of view and orbital geometry.
MERIS What is MERIS? A 15-band ocean-colour spectrometer aboard ESA's Envisat satellite, the direct predecessor of OLCI.
OLCI What is OLCI? A passive optical sensor aboard the European Sentinel-3A and -3B satellites, measuring reflected sunlight in 21 spectral bands from 400 to 1020 nm.
OLCI At what spatial resolution does OLCI image the ocean? 300 m.
L3 product What is an L3 satellite product? A satellite data level where individual swath measurements (L2) are re-projected onto a regular geographic grid and composited.
Neural network (ocean colour) What is a neural network in ocean-colour retrieval? A machine-learning algorithm trained on coincident in-situ chlorophyll measurements and satellite reflectance to retrieve chlorophyll-a and other variables.
Primitive equations What are the "primitive equations"? The set of partial differential equations that ocean general-circulation models like NEMO solve to evolve the three-dimensional state of the ocean.
NEMO What is NEMO? An open-source ocean modelling framework that solves the primitive equations of ocean dynamics on a 3D structured grid.
ERGOM What is ERGOM? A biogeochemical model originally developed at the Leibniz Institute for Baltic Sea Research (IOW) in Rostock and extended at BSH.
ERGOM Which CMEMS product uses ERGOM? BALTICSEA_ANALYSISFORECAST_BGC_003_007.
HYPE What is HYPE? A semi-distributed hydrological model developed by SMHI for simulating river discharge and nutrient loads from terrestrial catchments.
De Boyer Montégut method What does the De Boyer Montégut method compute? Mixed-layer depth (MLD) from a vertical profile of temperature or density.
Tier 2 — Mechanism & Process 65 cards
PromptAnswer
Practical Salinity Unit (PSU) How is PSU defined physically? From the electrical conductivity of seawater relative to a standard KCl solution at 15 °C and atmospheric pressure.
Adiabatic process What happens when a water parcel is lifted adiabatically toward the surface? It expands slightly and cools, with no heat exchanged with its surroundings.
Adiabatic process What is the conceptual purpose of the adiabatic correction in oceanography? To remove the pressure effect on temperature, converting in-situ temperature to potential temperature.
Potential temperature Why is potential temperature the standard thermodynamic variable in ocean models? For thermodynamic consistency when comparing temperatures across different pressure levels.
Wind stress How does wind stress drive ocean motion? It transfers momentum from air to water, generating surface currents and turbulent mixing in the mixed layer.
Wind stress What is Ekman transport and how is it caused? A Coriolis-deflected net transport of surface water set up by wind stress; it controls coastal upwelling and downwelling.
Convective cooling What kind of mixing does convective cooling generate? Buoyancy-driven vertical mixing as cooled, denser surface water sinks.
Convective cooling What two forces together deepen the mixed layer in autumn and winter? Wind stress and convective cooling.
Mixed layer How does mixed-layer depth control bloom dynamics? A shallow mixed layer (5–15 m) concentrates phytoplankton and nutrients in a thin sunlit zone, favouring rapid bloom growth.
Stratification How does strong stratification affect vertical mixing? It suppresses mixing, trapping surface nutrients and isolating cold, nutrient-rich deep water from sunlit layers.
Upwelling What typically drives upwelling? Wind-induced divergence of surface currents.
Upwelling How does upwelling affect algal blooms? By fertilising surface waters with nutrients from below, it can trigger or intensify blooms away from river inputs.
Sea-ice concentration How does sea ice influence the spring phytoplankton bloom? Ice cover limits light penetration and delays bloom onset; ice-melt timing is a strong predictor of bloom date.
Baltic Inflow (Major Baltic Inflow) What effect do Major Baltic Inflows have on deep-basin water? They ventilate anoxic bottom water, replenishing oxygen and temporarily halting sediment phosphorus release.
Baltic Inflow (Major Baltic Inflow) What conditions drive a Major Baltic Inflow? Sustained westerly winds combined with specific sea-level pressure conditions.
Nitrate (NO₃⁻) Why is winter surface nitrate predictive of the spring diatom bloom? The bloom consumes accumulated winter nitrate, so higher winter stocks fuel a larger bloom.
Phosphate (PO₄³⁻) Why does phosphate accumulate in Baltic bottom water during winter? Anoxic conditions release iron-bound phosphorus from sediments (internal loading), in addition to river inputs.
Silicate (Si(OH)₄) Why does silicate act as a "bloom-type switch" in the Baltic? It has no other major biological sink; once depleted, diatom growth stops and the community shifts to flagellates and cyanobacteria.
Internal loading How does internal loading work chemically? When bottom oxygen drops to zero, iron-phosphorus compounds in the sediment dissolve and phosphate diffuses upward into the water column.
Pigment What accessory pigment distinguishes cyanobacteria from other phytoplankton? Phycocyanin.
Pigment What accessory pigment is characteristic of dinoflagellates? Peridinin.
Biomass Why is satellite chlorophyll-a used as a biomass proxy? Direct biomass cannot be measured from space, but chlorophyll-a can — and it is present in all phytoplankton.
Taxonomy How is traditional phytoplankton taxonomy carried out? By microscopic identification of cell structure and morphology.
Protists Name some phototrophic protists. Diatoms, dinoflagellates, cryptophytes, and green algae.
Protists Name some heterotrophic protists. Ciliates, heterotrophic flagellates, and foraminifera.
Flagella (flagellum) How are dinoflagellate flagella arranged? Two flagella oriented perpendicular to each other — one transverse, one trailing.
Flagella (flagellum) How are cryptophyte flagella arranged? Two flagella of similar length emerging from a gullet-like groove.
Frustule How are the two halves of a frustule arranged? Like a Petri dish and its lid — an upper and a lower valve that fit together.
Phytoplankton What share of global oxygen production do phytoplankton account for? Roughly half.
Chlorophyll-a What wavelengths does chlorophyll-a absorb most strongly? Red (~665 nm) and blue (~443 nm), while reflecting green.
Phycocyanin At what wavelength does phycocyanin absorb characteristically? Around 620 nm.
Phytoplankton functional types (PFTs) What does PFT retrieval enable from satellite data? Bloom-type identification from ocean colour alone — e.g. diatom spring bloom vs. cyanobacteria summer bloom.
Picoplankton Why does picoplankton dominate in oligotrophic waters? Large cells cannot compete effectively in nutrient-poor conditions, so small cells with high surface-area-to-volume ratios win.
Microphytoplankton Why do microphytoplankton increase sediment oxygen demand? They sink quickly when they die, exporting organic carbon to depth and consuming bottom-water oxygen as they decompose.
Diatoms What ends a Baltic diatom bloom? Exhaustion of dissolved silicate, after which other phytoplankton groups take over.
Dinoflagellates What is a "red tide"? A dense red or brown surface discoloration of the water caused by dinoflagellate blooms.
Cryptophytes How do cryptophytes tolerate Baltic conditions? They handle low salinity and turbid coastal water well, and are identified by their distinctive accessory pigments.
Flagellates Which phytoplankton groups belong to the flagellates? Dinoflagellates, cryptophytes, and other motile algae.
Nitrogen fixation What enzyme catalyses nitrogen fixation? Nitrogenase, found in certain cyanobacteria and other bacteria.
Cyanotoxins How are cyanotoxins categorised? Into hepatotoxins (e.g. nodularin, microcystin) that attack the liver, and neurotoxins (e.g. anatoxin, saxitoxin) that attack the nervous system.
Zooplankton What two main processes terminate a phytoplankton bloom? Nutrient depletion and grazing by zooplankton.
Primary production How is net primary production (NPP) defined? Gross photosynthesis minus phytoplankton respiration.
Trophic state What is the standard trophic-state scale? Oligotrophic → mesotrophic → eutrophic → hypertrophic.
Oligotrophic Which phytoplankton size class thrives in oligotrophic waters? Picoplankton, which outcompete larger bloom-formers in nutrient-poor conditions.
Algal bloom What three conditions are needed for an algal bloom? Sufficient nutrients, adequate light, and favourable physical conditions such as warm temperature and stratification.
Summer bloom (cyanobacteria bloom) What physical conditions drive the Baltic summer cyanobacteria bloom? Warm temperatures, shallow thermal stratification, and elevated phosphate from internal loading.
Ciliates What do marine ciliates feed on? Nanophytoplankton and bacteria, occupying a trophic level between phytoplankton and crustacean zooplankton.
Wavelength (nanometre, nm) Why are CMEMS Rrs bands named by their centre wavelength? Each band targets a specific absorbing or scattering constituent of seawater (e.g. RRS443 = 443 nm).
Radiance & Irradiance Why is remote-sensing reflectance defined as a radiance-to-irradiance ratio? Dividing upwelling radiance by downwelling irradiance normalises out variation in the brightness of the sun.
Remote-sensing reflectance (Rrs) What does Rrs physically represent? The colour of the ocean as seen from space — different water constituents absorb and scatter different wavelengths.
Inherent Optical Properties (IOPs) What are the two fundamental IOPs? The absorption coefficient (a, m⁻¹) and the backscattering coefficient (b_bp, m⁻¹).
Backscattering What makes the particulate backscattering coefficient (BBP) high? Many suspended particles in the water — either living algae or inorganic sediment.
CDOM (Coloured Dissolved Organic Matter) At what wavelengths does CDOM absorb most strongly? In the blue-violet part of the spectrum.
Secchi depth How does Secchi depth relate to algal biomass? Inversely — high biomass means a shallow Secchi reading.
Band-ratio algorithm How does a band-ratio CHL algorithm work mechanically? The blue/green Rrs ratio shifts as chlorophyll absorbs more strongly in blue; the relationship is calibrated against in-situ measurements.
QAA (Quasi-Analytical Algorithm) How does QAA differ from band-ratio algorithms? QAA inverts the physics of underwater light propagation rather than fitting empirical band ratios.
In-situ measurement Give some examples of marine in-situ platforms. Shipboard CTDs, moored buoys, Argo floats, ferry-mounted sensors, and discrete water samples analysed in the lab.
Swath How are daily L3 composites built from satellite swaths? By remapping multiple swaths onto a fixed geographic grid and reducing them to a single per-pixel value (e.g. mean or maximum) per day.
OLCI Which ocean-colour sensor did OLCI replace? MERIS aboard ESA's Envisat.
L3 product How do L4 products differ from L3 products? L4 products take L3 further by gap-filling with statistical or model methods.
Primitive equations Which equations make up the "primitive equations"? Horizontal momentum (Navier–Stokes with rotation and gravity), mass continuity, thermodynamic energy, salt conservation, and an equation of state.
NEMO What ocean fields does NEMO simulate? Temperature, salinity, currents, sea-ice, and mixing.
ERGOM What biogeochemical cycles does ERGOM simulate? Nitrogen, phosphorus, silicate, and carbon cycles plus plankton dynamics in the Baltic Sea.
HYPE How does HYPE drive ERGOM? It supplies daily discharge and nitrogen/phosphorus/silicate loads as river boundary conditions to ERGOM.
De Boyer Montégut method How does the De Boyer Montégut method define mixed-layer depth? As the shallowest depth where potential density differs from the surface value by a fixed threshold, typically 0.03 kg m⁻³.

Tier 4 — Connections & Synthesis 39 cards
PromptAnswer
Practical Salinity Unit (PSU) Why is the Baltic salinity contrast the "master physical control" on stratification? Strong horizontal and vertical salinity gradients dominate Baltic density structure, more than temperature does.
Brackish water Why does Baltic brackish water force region-specific ocean-colour algorithms? Low salinity, high CDOM, and shallow depths combine to make the Baltic optically complex, breaking standard global algorithms.
Convective cooling How does spring surface heating reverse winter convection? It flips the sign of the surface buoyancy flux, re-establishing the seasonal stratification needed for bloom initiation.
Sea-ice concentration How does Baltic ice melt affect stratification? It delivers a pulse of fresh, low-density meltwater that strengthens stratification and primes the spring bloom.
Anoxia & Hypoxia What two anoxia-driven chemical changes sustain Baltic summer blooms? Sulfate reduction to H₂S, and dissolution of iron-bound phosphorus from sediments (internal loading).
Catchment (drainage basin) Why does the HYPE model bridge terrestrial land management and Baltic biogeochemistry? Because nutrient and CDOM loads reaching the Baltic originate from human land use within the catchment, which HYPE simulates daily.
