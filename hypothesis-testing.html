Hypotheses 4 cards
PromptAnswer
Null hypothesis What is the null hypothesis (H₀)? The formal statement being tested, typically proposing no difference or no effect. It is the claim assumed true while we look for evidence against it.
Alternative hypothesis What is the alternative hypothesis (H₁/Hₐ)? Our proposed explanation for what is happening if the null hypothesis is wrong.
Why do we test against the null rather than directly proving the alternative? The null is a single, precise claim (e.g. effect = 0) that fully specifies a sampling distribution, so we can calculate how probable our data are under it. The alternative is composite — 'effect ≠ 0' covers infinitely many values and specifies no single distribution — so there is nothing concrete to compute probabilities from. We can therefore only measure how improbable the data are under the precise null.
What does it mean to 'reject the null hypothesis'? The test statistic fell in a region so unlikely under H₀ (p-value ≤ α) that we treat H₀ as implausible and act as if the alternative holds. It is a decision, not a proof: it does not establish the alternative is true and carries a Type I error risk of α.
Test statistics 4 cards
PromptAnswer
Test statistic What is a test statistic? A single number computed from the data that captures the information relevant for choosing between the competing hypotheses.
How does the magnitude of a test statistic relate to evidence against H₀? Test statistics are scaled so that under H₀ they cluster near zero. A larger magnitude places the value farther into the tail of the null distribution, which corresponds to a smaller p-value — hence stronger evidence against H₀.
A Wald test statistic standardizes an estimate by dividing by its standard error: (estimate − hypothesized value) / standard error.
In a Wald test, what does the numerator (estimate − hypothesized value) represent? The signed distance between the observed estimate and the value the null hypothesis predicts. Dividing by the standard error expresses that distance in units of sampling variability.
P-values 5 cards
PromptAnswer
P-value What is a p-value? Assuming H₀ is true, the probability of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the one actually observed. A small value means the observed data would be surprising if the null were true.
Under H₀, what is the probability that the p-value falls below a threshold t? Exactly t. If the null is true, P(p-value ≤ t) = t for any threshold t — i.e. the p-value is uniformly distributed on [0,1].
Under H₀, why is the p-value uniformly distributed on [0,1]? The p-value is, by construction, the test statistic's tail probability under H₀ — i.e. its CDF (or survival function) evaluated at the observed value. By the probability integral transform, feeding a continuous random variable into its own CDF yields a Uniform(0,1). Hence P(p-value ≤ t) = t. (This requires a continuous null distribution; with discrete data the p-value is only approximately uniform.)
Does a small p-value mean the null hypothesis is false? No. It means the observed data would be unusual *if* the null were true. A small p-value can still occur when the null is true — that is a false rejection.
A small p-value is computed assuming the null hypothesis is true, so it tells us the data are improbable under H₀ — not that H₀ itself is improbable.
Significance & decisions 3 cards
PromptAnswer
Significance level What is the significance level (α)? A pre-specified probability threshold for the p-value, below which we reject H₀. It fixes the long-run Type I error rate — the fraction of true nulls we will falsely reject. The conventional value α = 0.05 is just that: a convention, with no objective justification.
What does 'statistically significant' at the 0.05 level mean? The p-value is 0.05 or smaller, which by convention is treated as sufficiently strong evidence against the null — nothing more fundamental than a chosen cutoff.
Why must the significance level be chosen *before* seeing the result? Pre-specifying α controls the false-rejection rate. Choosing the threshold after seeing the data lets you cherry-pick and inflates the true error rate.
Errors & power 6 cards
PromptAnswer
Type I error What is a Type I error? Rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true (a false positive). Its long-run rate equals the significance level α (e.g. 0.05).
Type II error What is a Type II error? Failing to reject the null hypothesis when the alternative is actually true (a false negative).
Type I error rate Which quantity equals the significance level α? The Type I error rate (probability of a false rejection).
Power What is the power of a test? The probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when the alternative is true — i.e. the probability of detecting a real effect.
What happens when a study has low power? It is unlikely to reject the null even when the alternative is true, so genuine effects often go undetected (a Type II error).
Power is the probability of rejecting H₀ when the alternative is true; a Type II error is failing to reject H₀ when the alternative is true.
Worked examples (test construction) 4 cards
PromptAnswer
How is a two-sample proportion test statistic constructed (e.g. comparing COVID risk perception across age groups)? Take the difference in sample proportions and divide it by the pooled standard error of that difference.
How is a comparison-of-means test statistic constructed (e.g. battery lifespans)? Take the difference in sample means and divide it by the combined standard error of the difference.
How is a test statistic for a correlation constructed (e.g. between two biomarkers)? Take the sample correlation coefficient and divide it by its standard error, approximately 1/√n.
What pattern do all these Wald-style examples share? Each takes an estimate of an effect and divides by its standard error, producing a standardized statistic whose null distribution is known.
One-sided vs two-sided tests 7 cards
PromptAnswer
Two-sided test What is a two-sided (two-tailed) test? A test whose alternative hypothesis allows the parameter to deviate from the null value in *either* direction (≠). Evidence in either tail counts against H₀.
One-sided test What is a one-sided (one-tailed) test? A test whose alternative specifies a single direction of deviation (> or <). Only evidence in that one tail counts against H₀.
How is the p-value computed differently for one-sided vs two-sided tests? A two-sided p-value sums the probability in both tails (often 2× the one-sided value for a symmetric null distribution); a one-sided p-value uses only the single tail matching the alternative's direction.
When is a one-sided test appropriate? Only when a deviation in the opposite direction is implausible or irrelevant, and the direction is chosen *before* seeing the data — never picked afterward to make a result significant.
Why is a one-sided test more 'powerful' for detecting an effect in its chosen direction? All of α is placed in one tail, so the rejection threshold is closer to the null value. The trade-off is zero ability to detect a true effect in the opposite direction.
A two-sided test rejects for deviations in either direction (Hₐ: θ ≠ θ₀), while a one-sided test rejects only for deviations in a single pre-specified direction (Hₐ: θ > θ₀ or θ < θ₀).
What is the danger of switching to a one-sided test after seeing the data? It is a form of p-hacking: choosing the direction post hoc roughly doubles the true Type I error rate, so the reported significance level no longer reflects the real false-positive risk.
Interpretation & history 3 cards
PromptAnswer
Who developed the formal hypothesis-testing framework, and roughly when? Ronald Fisher, Jerzy Neyman, and Egon Pearson, between about 1925 and 1940.
What criticism of formal hypothesis testing do the notes mention? Many prominent statisticians argue that less emphasis should be placed on formal hypothesis testing — e.g. over-reliance on the 0.05 cutoff and on binary significant/not-significant conclusions.
What is the key interpretive limitation of any hypothesis test? It can provide evidence against the null but cannot confirm the alternative is true, and every rejection carries a controlled but nonzero chance of being a false positive.
